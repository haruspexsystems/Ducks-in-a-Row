CRL monitoring

Know before a CRL expires, the offline root's included

A certificate that expires breaks one service. A certificate revocation list that expires breaks revocation checking for everything its CA ever signed, all at once: domain controllers, VPN and wireless authentication, and the CA's own service. Ducks in a Row watches every CRL in your CA's chain and warns you by email or webhook before one goes stale.

Download Read how it works

Why the offline root is the dangerous one

An issuing CA replaces its own CRL on a timer. An offline root's CRL is published by hand, expires on a schedule of its own, and nothing automatic replaces it. The root is switched off and easy to forget, so its CRL is the one nobody has in a calendar.

Every copy, watched separately

Ducks in a Row reads the connected CA's own CRLs from the CA itself and from the locations the CA tells its clients to read. It reads the CRL above it, on a two tier estate the offline root's, from the distribution points on the CA's certificate, over both LDAP and HTTP. Every copy is checked against the certificate of the CA that should have signed it, and one that does not verify is ignored.

Each copy is watched on its own. A root CRL renewed into the directory but never copied to the web server leaves every client that reads the HTTP location failing while the domain looks healthy. Ducks in a Row warns about the stale copy, names the location, and says that a newer CRL already exists elsewhere.

When it warns

The CRL isWhat you get
Replaced by a CA on a timerOne warning when the CA misses its own scheduled publication, and one when the CRL actually expires
Published by hand, such as an offline root'sWarnings 30, 14, 7 and 1 days before it expires by default, then one when it expires

A CRL on a timer gets no countdown, because a Windows CA's weekly CRL would trip it several times a week while the CA does exactly what it should. Publishing a new CRL resets the warnings. A distribution point that cannot be reached silences nothing: the warnings continue against the last copy read, and say when that was.

Where the warnings go

By email through your own SMTP relay, set up from the Settings page, and by webhook: an HTTP POST signed with HMAC-SHA256, with the events crl.expiring, crl.overdue and crl.expired. A CRL payload names the issuing CA, the CRL number, when it expires and every location it is served from. The Revocation lists card on the Settings page shows every CRL being watched and what was last read from each location.

Watch a production CA without writing to it

In monitor mode Ducks in a Row never writes to the CA: no ACME, no issuance, no revocation. The CA team grants a read right, and you get the certificate inventory, expiry alerts and CRL watching. Turning issuance on later is a rights grant, a registry value and a restart, never a reinstall.

CRL monitoring and monitor mode are part of the free core, on a production CA too. An installation connects to one CA and watches the CRLs in that CA's chain. Ducks in a Row is source available under the Business Source License 1.1, and it collects no telemetry.
