No quack remedies. Certificate management for ADCS that just goes with the flow.

Are you running Active Directory Certificate Services (ADCS) and still managing certificate renewals with improvised scripts and calendar reminders? Ducks in a Row is one Windows service in front of the ADCS certificate authority you already run. Your machines, domain joined or not, request and renew their own certificates from it with a standard ACME client.

Features

What you get

  • Expiry alerts, free

    Email and webhook alerts for every certificate in your CA's inventory.

  • Pull, not push

    Each machine asks for its own certificate, so no credentials for your servers are stored anywhere and Ducks in a Row needs no rights on them.

  • One scoped credential per team

    You set the names a team may request with an external account binding credential, and the team serves itself within them.

  • Your trust chain stays as it is

    Certificates come from the ADCS you already run and chain to the root you already trust.

  • Rights checked before first use

    The setup wizard proves what it can of the service's rights on your CA, and says plainly what it cannot.

  • Kubernetes too

    cert-manager's built in ACME issuer works with it, with no plugin in the cluster. The Kubernetes chapter says which parts our tests have run.

ACME server

Three steps to the first certificate

Ducks in a Row is an RFC 8555 ACME server that talks to your CA through the CA's own COM interface. Clients prove control of a name with http-01, dns-01 or tls-alpn-01, and managed devices with device-attest-01. External account binding keeps each team to its own names.

  1. Ask for the rights first

    One page lists every right and firewall opening the server needs, ready to forward to your AD and CA team. The approvals are usually the lead time.

  2. Install the signed setup

    It carries its own runtime, so a server with no internet access installs from the one file. Windows SmartScreen may still warn about a new release while the certificate builds a reputation, so check the download before you run it.

  3. Point your ACME clients at it

    The client guide covers certbot, win-acme, Caddy, Traefik, Posh-ACME and cert-manager, with the directory URL and the challenge types.

Certificate management

Your whole ADCS inventory in one place

The dashboard reads every certificate your CA has issued, not only the ones it issued over ACME, and keeps that inventory in step with the CA.

  • Totals and trends

    Certificates by status, certificates issued per day over the last 30 days, and the health of the fleet at a glance.

  • Search and filter

    Find a certificate in the inventory and narrow the list by status, from valid to expiring, expired and revoked.

  • Revoke within a scope you set

    Revoke a TLS certificate from the dashboard. By default only the certificates Ducks in a Row manages can be revoked, and you decide whether to widen that.

  • An audit trail

    Every change an administrator makes in the dashboard is recorded, with who made it and when.

CRL monitoring

The CRL nobody is paying attention to

A certificate that expires breaks one service. A certificate revocation list that expires breaks revocation checking for everything its CA ever signed, all at once, and for an offline root nothing automatic replaces it.

Ducks in a Row watches every CRL in the chain of the CA you connect it to, the offline root's included. It reads them from the CA itself and from the distribution points over LDAP and HTTP, and it watches each copy separately: a root CRL renewed into the directory but never copied to the web server is reported, with the location named.

Warnings for a CRL published by hand

  1. 30days
  2. 14days
  3. 7days
  4. 1day

A CRL published by hand gets warnings 30, 14, 7 and 1 days before it expires, by default. A CRL your CA replaces on a timer stays quiet unless the CA misses its own schedule, and warns again if it expires. The warnings go out by email and webhook, like certificate expiry, and they are part of the free core.

Privacy and sovereignty

No telemetry. Ever.

Ducks in a Row runs entirely on your own servers and never sends data back to us or to anyone else. Fonts and other assets are bundled with the app, so the dashboard makes no third party requests. A tool that sits in front of your certificate authority should not be watching you, and this one does not.

  • Sovereign by design

    Ducks in a Row is published by Haruspex Systems B.V., a company based in the Netherlands. It runs entirely on Windows servers you control, beside the CA you already run: no cloud service, no tenant and no account with us. The free core needs no licence key or activation, and the installer carries its own runtime, so it installs and runs on a network with no route to the internet. The source of the core is available, so you can read every line of it before it goes in front of your CA.

  • The Cyber Resilience Act

    The EU Cyber Resilience Act classes certificate issuance software as an important product, Class I. Its main obligations apply from 11 December 2027, and we are working to meet them before then. In place today: we report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents to ENISA as Article 14 requires, every release carries a software bill of materials, security fixes are promised until 31 December 2031, and vulnerabilities reach us through a published coordinated disclosure policy.
